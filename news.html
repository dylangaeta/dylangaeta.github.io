Milestone #

Defended my Ph.D. dissertation at Johns Hopkins University

Defended my dissertation at Johns Hopkins University: U.S. emissions of trace gas air pollutants inferred from a long-term atmospheric measurement network: from fumigants to fossil fuels. Three case studies use NOAA flask measurements and a geostatistical inverse model to map U.S. emissions:

California dominates U.S. emissions of sulfuryl fluoride, mostly from structural fumigation for drywood termites. Methyl bromide emissions fell after the Montreal Protocol phase-out but not to zero, sustained by quarantine and pre-shipment uses and California strawberry growing. Oil and gas basins, led by the Permian, Anadarko, Eagle Ford, and Haynesville, dominate U.S. propane, butane, and pentane emissions, with implications for ozone downwind.

November 13, 2025  ·  Johns Hopkins University

Service & outreach #

Testified at the EPA's public hearing on the Affordable Clean Energy rule in Chicago

Testified at the EPA's only public hearing on the proposed "Affordable Clean Energy" rule, the replacement for the Clean Power Plan. Speaking as an independent citizen scientist, I argued against the rule: energy infrastructure built today lasts for decades, and the clean energy transition is the moon race of my generation.

Read my testimony

My name is Dylan Gaeta. I am a trained Earth/climate scientist and I would like to come on the record as an independent; I do not wish to represent an organization or institution, but simply speak as a concerned citizen scientist.

With that being said, I would like to give myself a little bit of credibility. I have a Master’s degree in Geophysical Sciences from the University of Chicago. I’ve extensively studied the fundamental physics and chemistry that govern the behavior of Earth’s climate, atmosphere, and biogeochemical cycles, and I wrote my M.S. thesis on a model that describes the human impact on Earth’s carbon cycle; a model that I built under the mentorship of a UChicago professor, and continue to improve today.

I am here as a somewhat younger citizen of our country to echo the sentiments of many other millennials. I am 24 years old. I have many more years left on this Earth than nearly all of the people who will be making the decision about whether to repeal the Clean Power Plan. I would like to respectfully ask those people to strongly consider the testimonies of people my age, and to consider the quality of the air that myself and my children will breathe should the Clean Power Plan be repealed.

We know from looking at our coal and nuclear plants that energy infrastructure lasts several decades, and the decisions that are made today about how to generate electricity for our country will last as long as the infrastructure remains functional. I vehemently oppose the “Affordable Clean Energy” plan because it does not provide an incentive for our energy sector to build the infrastructure to generate carbon-free energy, and in fact it would allow the production of coal-derived energy to increase. (I would ask anyone who claims that coal can be “clean” whether they would be comfortable lighting a coal fire in an indoor fireplace with your newborn baby sleeping in the room, every single day.)

As the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) and numerous other independent groups of Earth scientists, economists, and public policy experts have made abundantly clear, it requires concerted global action of all major nations to achieve the carbon dioxide and methane emission rates required to keep the globally-reaching impacts of climate change below a specific level of damage. The “Affordable Clean Energy” plan enables states and individual power plants to effectively withdraw from the stated emission goals of the U.S., which immediately compromises the “concerted global” component of “it requires concerted global action.”

I can tell you from personal interactions with my Chinese colleagues that millennial-aged scientists, engineers, and policy makers in China are prepared to lead the world in the challenge to reduce and ultimately eliminate human dependence on coal, oil, natural gas, and other fossil fuels. They recognize that the United States is faltering on this front, and they are preparing to take the reins as the world-leader in carbon-free energy.

The global clean energy challenge is the moon race of our generation. The race to eliminate health-damaging pollutants from our energy system, and to build technologies and infrastructure that will allow us to supply energy to all U.S. residents without releasing carbon dioxide into the atmosphere and ocean, is as important to us as beating the Soviet Union to the moon was to the U.S. citizens of the 50’s and 60’s.

I strongly oppose the administration’s proposed “Affordable Clean Energy” plan. The reasons I have stated all coincide with the stated objectives of the EPA to protect human health and the environment. It is not the EPA’s job to protect the coal industry. Furthermore, my reasoning is deeply rooted in a desire to see the United States rise to the top of the global stage on energy and environment issues, which, again, are the defining global issues of my generation. I am testifying today because I believe that my country has the ability to rise to the top of this challenge, but only if we treat it with the same urgency as we treated the race to the moon.

Thank you for the opportunity to testify.

Dylan C. Gaeta

October 1, 2018  ·  Ralph H. Metcalfe Federal Building, Chicago, IL
